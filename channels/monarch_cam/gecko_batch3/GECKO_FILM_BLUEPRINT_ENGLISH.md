## Original narration — historical user-approved recording text

This retains the existing draft verbatim to preserve the user's previous approval of its English voice recording; it is not an assertion that every sentence has passed the present science review. The release passage is a **material wording review hold** (below); if changed, the affected recording must be regenerated or edited and approved, not silently presented as the approved original.

> A gecko can hold onto a wall. So how does it let go with every step?
>
> The secret isn't just a strong grip. It's how that grip is controlled.
>
> Under a Tokay gecko's toes are tiny, hair-like structures called setae. Their branching tips make extremely close contact with the surface.
>
> They aren't suction cups. At that tiny scale, van der Waals forces help those tips adhere.
>
> But direction matters. A pull toward the base of the toe, along the surface, helps support the grip.
>
> To release, the toe starts curling back at its tip. Contact progressively breaks from the tip toward the base, rather than the whole pad lifting at once.
>
> That toe movement is called digital hyperextension. Don't confuse it with the angles of the microscopic hairs beneath it.
>
> So a gecko doesn't simply yank its foot off the wall. Close contact, the direction of loading, and controlled toe movement work together to make gripping—and letting go—possible.

**Scientific release hold:** The two absolute sentences in paragraph six describe a clean tip-to-base sequence as universal. Digital hyperextension and directional attachment are supported, but the precise contact front and how detachment works across structures and circumstances must not be represented as a universally verified simple tape peel. Before final recording/edit lock, verify that wording against the primary sources and soften it if needed. A proposed replacement, **not in the approved recording**, is: “To release, a gecko can curl its toes away from the surface. This movement helps reduce contact and lets the foot detach.” If adopted, also review the following “That toe movement” and recording continuity. The tip-to-base visualization can be labelled an illustrative example only if supported for that particular movement, not a quantified force simulation.

## Claim/source map and visual limits

- **C1 — Tokay / hierarchy:** Tian et al. (2006), [Adhesion and friction in gecko toe attachment and detachment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1748224/), describes setal/spatular structures and their functional role. Do not imply all geckos have adhesive pads; leopard geckos lack them ([San Diego Zoo](https://animals.sandiegozoo.org/animals/leopard-gecko)). Tokay identification requires an appropriately credible model.
- **C2 — close contact and intermolecular forces:** Tian et al. (2006), above. Illustrate as an explanation, not a microscope image or a calculation of force. Not suction or glue.
- **C3 — load direction:** Autumn et al. (2006), [Frictional adhesion: a new angle on gecko attachment](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/209/18/3569/16118/Frictional-adhesion-a-new-angle-on-gecko), describes directional/shear-dependent attachment. Avoid implying one fixed angle acts as a universal switch.
- **C4 — detachment / morphology:** Prior work mapped toe movement to Higham/Russell, DOI [10.1242/jeb.247980](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.247980), Hu et al. DOI [10.1098/rsif.2012.0108](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2012.0108), and Tokay foot anatomy to Russell et al. DOI [10.1111/joa.70251](https://doi.org/10.1111/joa.70251). Do not overstate the contact sequence; resolve the release-wording hold before recording lock. Prior figures were reference only; rights for reproduction were not cleared.
