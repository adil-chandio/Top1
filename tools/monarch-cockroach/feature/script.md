## 2. Scene-by-Scene `16:9` Script Booklet (`SCRIPT V2` · `7` Chapters · `30` Paragraphs)

### SCENE 01 · `0:00–0:30` (`+3 s` hold) · **Under the Door** *(Cold Open)*
> Flip on a light, and a cockroach bolts for the crack under a closed door.
>
> Standing up, it's half an inch tall. That gap ahead is barely as thick as two stacked pennies.
>
> It doesn't slow down.
>
> Its head dips in. Its back flattens.
>
> And in under a second, it's gone.
>
> Wait a second. An insect's shell is supposed to be stiff armor. Squashed that flat, its feet can't even stand underneath it. So how does stiff armor fold in half—and what pushes it through?

- **PICTURE ONLY:** Open at cockroach eye height (`12.52 mm` standing *Periplaneta americana*) sprinting across `40`-grit warm floor toward a `3.2 mm` clear acrylic slot beside two stacked US pennies (`~3.04 mm`). Show the sub-second vanish before the title card (`How This Cockroach Runs Without Its Feet`); hold back the macro shin-spine strike for Scene 04.

---

### SCENE 02 · `0:30–1:28` (`+4 s` hold) · **Five Moves Through the Slot**
> At real speed, your eyes miss almost everything. So researchers filmed American cockroaches at five hundred frames per second. Getting through wasn't one smooth slide. It took five separate moves.
>
> First, the antennae sweep the slot. Next, the head tips down and wedges in. Then the shield behind the head rolls slightly to slip inside. Meanwhile, the back legs stay planted outside and shove the chest through.
>
> Only at the end does the flattened belly pull clear. Watch the back plates during that squeeze. The shell isn't bending like soft rubber.
>
> Instead, stiff plates overlap like roof tiles. Soft hinge tissue between those plates folds nearly in half. The body flattens without cracking a single plate.

- **PICTURE ONLY:** `500 fps` slowed side + `3/4` reconstruction of all `5` stages (`1` antennal sweep → `2` head wedge → `3` slight pronotum roll → `4` planted external hind-leg thorax shove → `5` compressed abdomen exit). Match-cut into a clean sagittal diagram: rigid amber tergite plates keep constant length while soft cyan arthrodial hinges fold (`~47%` compression). Minimal super: `Rigid chitin plates · Soft arthrodial hinges`.

### SCENE 03 · `1:28–2:24` (`+4 s` hold) · **The Low-Ceiling Surprise**
> Still, a doorway is only half the puzzle. Outside that slot, the back legs had room to push. What happens when the whole ceiling stays low, with nowhere to stand?
>
> In the lab, cockroaches raced down a low corridor. Step by step, researchers dropped the roof closer to the floor. Cut the height in half—right onto its back—and you'd expect it to slow down.
>
> It didn't. Down to half its standing height, the cockroach ran at nearly full speed. Its legs simply splayed wider, keeping the same fast beat on every stride.
>
> Then the roof dropped just two millimeters lower, squeezing the body itself. Speed crashed to a quarter of its sprint. Its normal three-legged running rhythm fell apart.

- **PICTURE ONLY:** Let the visual `SUPER` gauge carry exact lab numbers (`15 cm corridor · 12 mm → 9 mm → 6 mm: ~58 cm/s · 61 ms stride` vs `4 mm: 14.5 cm/s`) so the voice-over stays completely clean of table clutter. Synchronized side + overhead lanes make the `4 mm` `4×` cliff unmistakable.

---

### SCENE 04 · `2:24–3:28` (`+6 s` hold) · **When Shins Become Feet** *(Uninterrupted Contact Proof)*
> Why did two millimeters break its stride? And how was it still moving at all? Follow one back leg from behind as the roof comes down.
>
> With room overhead, the cockroach runs high on the tips of its feet. Tiny hooked claws and soft pads catch the ground on every step.
>
> Now press the ceiling down until the body flattens. Forced out past eighty degrees, the leg tips sideways. Picture trying a push-up with your arms stretched straight out to the sides. Those foot claws lift off their normal angle.
>
> Look higher up the leg instead, along the shin. Rows of stiff, backward-pointing spines press straight into the floor. As the leg drives back, those shin spines dig in like crampons.
>
> Overhead, the rhythm shifts too. Both middle legs often shove together like a pair of oars. Back rubbing the roof, belly rubbing the floor, each spiny shin pushes with five times the insect's weight.

- **PICTURE ONLY:** Uninterrupted rear/macro camera on the right hind leg from `12 mm` down to `4 mm` (`>80°` sprawl): distal claws tip sideways (`+0.47 mm` clear of floor) while `3` stiff tibial spines plant at `z = 0.00 mm` and drive rearward without slip. Cut overhead only after contact to show synchronous middle-leg oar shoves (`~5×` BW/leg). Zero text overlays over the spine strike.

### SCENE 05 · `3:28–4:24` (`+4 s` hold) · **The Rough-Floor Trap**
> Could the splayed feet still be helping out of sight? Researchers measured leg grip without the feet engaging at all. On rough ground, the spiny shins alone still locked in tight.
>
> That sets up an obvious bet. If shin spines need grit to catch, then a rougher floor should always make it faster. Right?
>
> Across more than six hundred confined runs, researchers tested slick, medium, and coarse surfaces. Rougher ceilings always slowed the insect down. Overhead rub only fights forward motion.
>
> On the floor, though, the coarsest grit slowed it down too. On slick ground, the shin spines slipped early. On very coarse ground, the spines gripped hard—but the flattened belly snagged on every grain.

- **PICTURE ONLY:** Represent spine-only grip via clean force-diagram callouts (never reenact surgical tarsal ablation). Then animate the `638`-trial `4 mm` matrix: ceiling roughness monotonically slows speed (`6.64 → 5.63 → 0.36 cm/s`), while floor speed peaks at medium `40`-grit (`5.26 cm/s`) and drops on coarse `20`-grit (`3.69 cm/s`) as ventral belly drag (`μ_g = 0.76`) snags.

---

### SCENE 06 · `4:24–5:20` (`+3 s` hold) · **Tough Shell, Tight Limit**
> In separate compression tests, those overlapping plates took hundreds of times the cockroach's weight. Brief peaks reached roughly nine hundred times its weight. Spreading load across hinged plates let the insects sprint away at normal speed afterward.
>
> Armor that tough, plus shins like crampons, makes it sound unstoppable. So return to that narrowing doorway, across three hundred and fifteen lab attempts.
>
> At wider gaps, nearly three out of four attempts shot straight through. Narrow that slot to two stacked pennies—about three millimeters—and more than four out of five failed.
>
> Only seventeen percent made it through. Most swept the slot with their antennae, felt the squeeze, and turned back. Nearly one in ten wedged fast at the thickest part of its chest.

- **PICTURE ONLY:** First show the `~300×` / `~900×` BW distributed load (`~3 cm²` contact patch, `2 mm` safety stop) as a clean engineering force diagram followed by the unimpaired `76.7 cm/s` post-test sprint. Then cut back to the narrowing gate (`315` trials): `72%` pass at `6.1` & `4.4 mm` crashing to `17%` at `3.2 mm` (`74%` antennal turn-back, `9%` thorax-wedged, no gore).

---

### SCENE 07 · `5:20–6:00` (`+8 s` hold) · **Hinges and Spines**
> Now watch that two-penny escape once more at real speed. Antennae read the slot, stiff plates tile down on soft hinges, and spiny shins drive it clear.
>
> Past the far edge, those folded hinges spring the shell back to half an inch. Its feet take over the sprint again.
>
> Solid armor would jam at the edge, and feet alone would fail inside. It doesn't vanish under a door by being indestructible. It gets through because its shell can fold—and its shins can walk.

- **PICTURE ONLY:** Natural `1×` real-speed replay (`< 1 s` traversal) beside the two pennies, viscoelastic hinge rebound back to `12.52 mm`, tarsal claws re-engaging the floor, and an `8`-second quiet hold on the empty `3.2 mm` slot before readable primary `PNAS` credits. **No spoken CTA.**
