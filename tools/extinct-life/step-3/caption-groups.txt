# 01
Three-toed dinosaur tracks in Brazil.
More in Cameroon,|about six thousand kilometres away.
Today, the Atlantic lies between them.
But the ocean came later.
Rewind the map and those tracks|are on the same landmass.
Could dinosaurs have moved|from one region to the other?
The rocks around the prints hold clues.

# 02
Go back to the Early Cretaceous,
more than a hundred and twenty|million years ago.
South America and Africa were already|pulling apart in places,
but the land in this region|was still joined.
It was part of western Gondwana.
The researchers estimate|that the track regions were about
a thousand kilometres apart then.
Still a long way,|but no ocean stood between them.

# 03
That makes a crossing possible.
It doesn't tell us which animal|went where.
Even the same foot can leave|a crisp print in firm mud
and a blurred one|where the edges slump.
Two different animals can also leave|deceptively similar marks.
So to understand the tracks,
you need to look at|what they were pressed into

# 04
At a site in Cameroon's Koum Basin,|tracks occur on four distinct surfaces.
A recent study examined|hundreds of prints there.
One three-toed impression|is about sixty-nine centimetres long.
The team thinks|a large theropod made it.
But the marks around the prints|are just as revealing.
Ripples show where water moved.
Cracks show where mud dried.
Under a microscope,|the rock holds clues
that thin mats of microbes|helped keep the mud in place.
That may be why some tracks|are still so sharp.
The rocks record|shallow rivers and lakes
that went through wet and dry spells.

# 05
In Brazil's Sousa Basin,|dinosaur tracks also survive
in deposits from ancient|rivers and lakes.
Both regions have basins that formed|as the crust stretched
and shifted along faults.
Those low areas collected|the sediment that kept the tracks.
Fossils in the region, including pollen|in some of the surrounding deposits,
help researchers put the rocks|in a broad Early Cretaceous window.
That's different from dating|a single step.
The prints in Brazil and Cameroon|might be separated
by a great deal of time,|even when they look alike.

# 06
Put those clues back on the old map.
The land was connected,|and both regions preserve signs
of places where dinosaurs|walked beside water.
Researchers propose that valleys|in this part of Gondwana
gave land animals a way|to spread between the regions.
Ancient rivers may have helped|link those habitats.
But this isn't a trail of prints|all the way from one side to the other.
It's evidence for a route|that could have existed.

# 07
As the plates kept moving apart,|the land connection was lost
and the Atlantic widened.
The rocks moved with the plates,|and so did every footprint they held.
The impressions are still there.
A track can tell you|where a dinosaur put its foot.
To see where it could have gone next,|you need the rocks around that print
and a map of a world long gone.
