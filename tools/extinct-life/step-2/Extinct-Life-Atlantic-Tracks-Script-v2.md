## 01 · The ocean came later · 0:00–0:36

**Narration**

Three-toed dinosaur tracks in Brazil. More in Cameroon. Look up the two places today and there are about six thousand kilometres between them, with the Atlantic in the way.

But rewind the map. That stretch of ocean is gone. The two regions are about a thousand kilometres apart, on the same ancient landmass. The ocean between them came later.

That gives a dinosaur somewhere to walk. It doesn't give us a trail. To see why researchers think there was a route here, start in Cameroon, where one stretch of rock holds four different surfaces that dinosaurs walked on.

## 02 · Four pieces of ancient ground · 0:36–1:39

**Narration**

In northern Cameroon, the bed of the Mayo Rey River exposes rocks of the Koum Basin. At one site, researchers studied two hundred and eighty-three footprints. A series of steps left by a single animal is called a trackway. The team documented thirty-four trackways. With a sequence, you can work out which way an animal was moving on that patch of ground. Once the prints stop, so does the record of its steps.

And the tracks aren't all on the same surface. Peel back the layers and you find different pieces of ancient ground, each exposed long enough for something to walk on it. That record is still changing today. Erosion has broken up parts of the site, and some of the tracks from earlier surveys are no longer visible.

To record the surfaces in three dimensions, the team took more than two thousand overlapping photographs. That preserves the shape of the slabs they modelled, even if the originals wear away. Among the prints they measured is a three-toed impression about sixty-nine centimetres long.

**Picture:** Label **KOUM BASIN · CAMEROON**. Stack four original bedding diagrams and isolate each surface; use a separate schematic to count individual steps versus a trackway. Briefly turn overlapping view cards into a neutral shaded relief labelled **HOW PHOTOGRAMMETRY WORKS**. No journal mesh or exact site replica. Save the whole ruler for the next beat. [M26]

## 03 · The big print has a problem · 1:39–2:32

**Narration**

Sixty-nine centimetres. That's the length of the impression, not the height of the animal. The researchers think a large theropod made it. But one of the most useful things about this print is that its edges aren't sharp. Softer sediment may have deformed around the foot, leaving a much less distinct outline.

Here's why that matters. Press the same three-toed shape into two patches of mud. One patch holds the edges. In the other, the walls slump in as you lift the foot. Now you have two marks that look different, made by the same foot. At Koum, researchers describe sequences where clear prints give way to muddled ones as the ground changes. And the reverse problem is just as real: different animals can leave similar three-toed tracks. To compare the prints in Brazil and Cameroon, you have to compare what was under their feet.

**Picture:** Ruler only for **~69 CM · ONE IMPRESSION**; don't substitute another specimen's photo. Same generic stamp, same view, two substrates; draw crisp edges first and collapse second, not two different dinosaurs. Keep **ILLUSTRATION** visible. The fuzzy margin is documented; this is not a recreation of that exact footprint. [M26 fig. 5F; J24]

## 04 · What the mud held onto · 2:32–3:34

**Narration**

Look past the toes. Ripples in the rock mark places where water moved. The mud cracks tell you the surface dried. You can have both clues in the same stack of rock because the conditions didn't stay the same. A shallow margin could go from covered with water to exposed ground. That's when an animal could add its own mark.

Then comes the harder part. A print left this afternoon could be gone by tomorrow. Why did these last? Under a microscope, thin slices of rock show fine grains held together by a mineral cement. The researchers took that as a sign that some of the sediment hardened early. They also found clues that thin mats of microbes helped bind the grains together. A mat could help the edge of a print hold instead of crumbling. If fresh sediment buried the surface before it eroded, the mark had a chance of lasting. The dinosaur made the step. The mud, and what lived on it, helped make it a fossil.

**Picture:** Follow a stylized print outward into ripple ridges, then cut to polygonal dried mud. Pass through a schematic thin section to a magnified mat/particle diagram. Close on a burial cross-section, with the depression filled by later sediment. Portray a possible preservation sequence, not a time-lapse of TK-034 or a real micrograph. [M26 figs. 5, 8 and sedimentology]

## 05 · The other side of the comparison · 3:34–4:32

**Narration**

Now cross the modern Atlantic to Brazil's Sousa Basin. Here, the rocks also record shallow lakes and rivers. In a study of the Brazilian tracks, researchers found textures they attributed to microbial mats. At one site, you can see raised rims where the foot pushed sediment out around the print. The team argues that a more cohesive surface helped those rims and the impressions survive.

So the resemblance isn't just in the toes. In both regions there were places where a dinosaur could step onto damp ground, and conditions that could keep a record of it. That's a better comparison than holding up two silhouettes. But it also raises a problem. A riverbank in one place can look like a riverbank somewhere else, even if the two were never linked. And two tracks sitting side by side on a screen might be separated by millions of years. So how do you tell if they belonged to a connected landscape?

**Picture:** Cut from the Koum schematic to a separately composed Sousa scene. Highlight the raised rim around a generic print rather than showing a “match.” Present two rock columns with deliberately unmatched horizons; transition from the columns to age bands. [C13; J24; M26]

## 06 · Date the rocks, then move the continents · 4:32–5:31

**Narration**

First, you give the rocks a window of time. A distinctive kind of fossil pollen can help tie a layer to rocks of known age elsewhere. But here, many of the age clues come from other layers in the region. The two track beds don't carry the same date stamp. The tracks are broadly Early Cretaceous. They aren't dated closely enough to say the dinosaurs were walking at the same time.

Then you restore the geography. This isn't done by lining up the toes, or by sliding today's beaches together. The comparison goes down into older bedrock and the faults running through it. In the 2024 reconstruction, those geological structures link the regions before separation. As the crust stretched, parts of it sank along faults. The resulting low areas collected sand and mud from rivers and lakes. Those basins survived as separate pieces of the rock record. Put them back in their ancient positions and the land between them starts to matter.

**Picture:** Pollen is a clue from surrounding regional deposits, not an exact KB-17 date. Use broad Early Cretaceous bands without forcing them to coincide. Turn one labelled generic fault block into a sediment-catching basin; don't map an unnamed fault as the direct footpath. [J24; M26; KS21]

## 07 · A route, not a trail · 5:31–6:47

**Narration**

A land connection on a map says an ocean wasn't in the way. It doesn't tell you where an animal would find water or where it could move through the landscape. The rock record puts dinosaurs near rivers and lakes in both regions. The researchers propose that valleys in this part of Gondwana could have linked those habitats. Populations could spread along such a route over generations without any one animal walking from one end to the other.

There's even a clue that didn't leave footprints. Fossils of a kind of coelacanth called Mawsonia have turned up in other basins on both sides. The researchers compare those finds with their model of the old river system. The fish don't trace a single river any more than two sets of prints trace a dinosaur's journey. But the fossils give scientists another pattern to test against the geography.

What would make the case stronger? More sites in between, with narrower dates and fossils that let you identify more of the animals. Then you could start to work out when and where different populations might have spread. For now the evidence supports a possible route. We do know what happened to the land connection. And that's where the ocean comes back.

**Picture:** Highlight a broad **POSSIBLE DISPERSAL AREA** over the ancient map, not a line or arrow of migration. Cut to a labelled fish schematic and **FOSSILS IN OTHER BASINS**; don't put Mawsonia at either footprint locality. Keep the inferred river network schematic, not a surveyed line. [J24, “Rivers as Dispersal Routes” and fig. 7]

## 08 · The journey we can follow · 6:47–7:24

**Narration**

The connection didn't vanish in a single split. The Atlantic opened in stages. As the plates separated, the land connection in this region was lost and the ocean widened. Put today's map back on screen and there's that six-thousand-kilometre gap again.

So what made the journey we can actually follow? Not a dinosaur. The rocks. The footprints stayed in the beds that held them while the land on either side moved apart. Find one print and you know where a foot came down. Put that print back in its landscape and you can start to see where life could have gone next.

**Picture:** Hold the ancient model, then cut to modern geography; no sped-up rift racing under a dinosaur. Let the two pins stay distinct through the last line. Then give source credits rather than a second recap. [J24; KS21]
