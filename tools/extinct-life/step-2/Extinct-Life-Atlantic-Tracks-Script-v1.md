## 0:00–0:26 · An ocean between them

**Narration**

Three-toed dinosaur tracks in Brazil. More in Cameroon, about six thousand kilometres away. Today, the Atlantic lies between them. But the ocean came later. Rewind the map and those tracks are on the same landmass. Could dinosaurs have moved from one region to the other? The rocks around the prints hold clues.

## 1:26–2:12 · Four surfaces, hundreds of prints

**Narration**

At a site in Cameroon's Koum Basin, tracks occur on four distinct surfaces. A recent study examined hundreds of prints there. One three-toed impression is about sixty-nine centimetres long. The team thinks a large theropod made it. But the marks around the prints are just as revealing. Ripples show where water moved. Cracks show where mud dried. Under a microscope, the rock holds clues that thin mats of microbes helped keep the mud in place. That may be why some tracks are still so sharp. The rocks record shallow rivers and lakes that went through wet and dry spells.

## 2:12–2:57 · Compare the rocks, not just the toes

**Narration**

In Brazil's Sousa Basin, dinosaur tracks also survive in deposits from ancient rivers and lakes. Both regions have basins that formed as the crust stretched and shifted along faults. Those low areas collected the sediment that kept the tracks. Fossils in the region, including pollen in some of the surrounding deposits, help researchers put the rocks in a broad Early Cretaceous window. That's different from dating a single step. The prints in Brazil and Cameroon might be separated by a great deal of time, even when they look alike.

## 2:57–3:34 · A route animals could use

**Narration**

Put those clues back on the old map. The land was connected, and both regions preserve signs of places where dinosaurs walked beside water. Researchers propose that valleys in this part of Gondwana gave land animals a way to spread between the regions. Ancient rivers may have helped link those habitats. But this isn't a trail of prints all the way from one side to the other. It's evidence for a route that could have existed.

## 3:34–4:10 · The rocks carried the record

**Narration**

As the plates kept moving apart, the land connection was lost and the Atlantic widened. The rocks moved with the plates, and so did every footprint they held. The impressions are still there. A track can tell you where a dinosaur put its foot. To see where it could have gone next, you need the rocks around that print and a map of a world long gone.
